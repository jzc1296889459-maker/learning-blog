## Preliminaries: measure-theoretic probability

A probability space is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$:

- $\Omega$ is the set of possible outcomes.
- $\mathcal{F}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of measurable events, closed under complements and countable
  unions.
- $\mathbb{P}$ is a probability measure on those events.

A random variable is a measurable map $X : \Omega \to \mathcal{X}$. Its distribution (or
law) is the pushforward measure

$$
\mu_X(A) = \mathbb{P}(X \in A), \qquad A \subseteq \mathcal{X}.
$$

For an integrable function $f$, expectation is an integral with respect to a probability
measure:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
= \int_\Omega f(X(\omega))\,\mathbb{P}(d\omega)
= \int_\mathcal{X} f(x)\,\mu_X(dx).
$$

If $X$ has finite or countable support, the same integral is a sum:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)\,\mathbb{P}(X=x).
$$

A probability mass function or density $p(x)$ is a representation of a measure relative to
counting or Lebesgue measure; not every probability measure needs to have a density. Statements
that hold outside a set of probability zero hold **almost surely**, abbreviated a.s.

### Transition kernels and measures on paths

Let $(\mathcal{X},\mathscr{X})$ and $(\mathcal{Y},\mathscr{Y})$ be measurable spaces. A
**stochastic kernel** $K$ from $\mathcal{X}$ to $\mathcal{Y}$ assigns a probability distribution
$K(x,\cdot)$ on $\mathcal{Y}$ to every $x\in\mathcal{X}$, with two requirements:

1. $B\mapsto K(x,B)$ is a probability measure for each fixed $x$.
2. $x\mapsto K(x,B)$ is measurable for each fixed $B\in\mathscr{Y}$.

We often write $K(x,dy)$. A Markov transition kernel is simply a stochastic kernel from the
current state space to the next state space.

On a finite space, define $K(x,y)=K(x,\{y\})$. Then

$$
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}K(x,y)=1,
\qquad
\int_\mathcal{Y}g(y)K(x,dy)
=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}g(y)K(x,y).
$$

An initial distribution and a sequence of kernels determine a probability measure on an infinite
product space. More precisely, let $\mu_0$ be a distribution on $\mathcal{X}_0$, and let

$$
K_t(x_0,\ldots,x_t,dx_{t+1})
$$

be a kernel that selects the next coordinate given the history. The Ionescu–Tulcea theorem gives
a unique probability measure $\mathbb{P}$ on
$\Omega=\prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{X}_t$ whose probabilities on cylinder events are obtained by
iterated integration:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}(X_0\in A_0,\ldots,X_n\in A_n) \\
&\quad=
\int_{A_0}\mu_0(dx_0)
\int_{A_1}K_0(x_0,dx_1)\cdots
\int_{A_n}K_{n-1}(x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1},dx_n).
\end{aligned}
$$

For a time-homogeneous Markov chain, the next-state kernel depends only on the current state:
$K_t(x_0,\ldots,x_t,dx_{t+1})=K(x_t,dx_{t+1})$. On a finite state space this reduces to

$$
\mathbb{P}(X_0=x_0,\ldots,X_n=x_n)
=\mu_0(x_0)\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}K(x_t,x_{t+1}).
$$

In RL, a policy $\pi(da\mid s)$ and an environment kernel $P(ds'\mid s,a)$ alternate:

$$
\mu_0(ds_0)\,
\pi(da_0\mid s_0)\,
P(ds_1\mid s_0,a_0)\,
\pi(da_1\mid s_1)\,
P(ds_2\mid s_1,a_1)\cdots.
$$

For finite state and action spaces, the probability of a finite trajectory prefix is therefore

$$
\mathbb{P}_{\mu_0}^{\pi}
(S_0=s_0,A_0=a_0,\ldots,A_{n-1}=a_{n-1},S_n=s_n)
=\mu_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}
\pi(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t).
$$

Their iterated integrals define a unique probability law
$\mathbb{P}_{\mu_0}^{\pi}$ on trajectories
$\tau=(S_0,A_0,S_1,A_1,\ldots)$. This is the object hidden by informal expressions such as
$\tau\sim p_\pi$ or $\mathbb{E}_\pi$.

### Conditional probability and conditional expectation

Let $X$ be integrable and let $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ represent the information currently
available. A conditional expectation $Z=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is a
$\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable satisfying

$$
\int_G Z\,d\mathbb{P}=\int_G X\,d\mathbb{P}
\qquad\text{for every }G\in\mathcal{G}.
$$

It is unique up to almost-sure equality. On the standard Borel spaces normally used in RL, we can
also use a regular conditional distribution $K_{X\mid Y}(y,dx)$. For suitable $g$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid Y]
=\int_\mathcal{X}g(x)K_{X\mid Y}(Y,dx).
$$

When $X$ is discrete, this becomes the familiar conditional sum

$$
\mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid Y=y]
=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}g(x)\mathbb{P}(X=x\mid Y=y).
$$

The rules used most often are:

- **Linearity:**
  $\mathbb{E}[aX+bY\mid\mathcal{G}]
  =a\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]+b\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]$.
- **Taking out what is known:** if $Y$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, then
  $\mathbb{E}[YX\mid\mathcal{G}]=Y\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ whenever the terms are integrable.
- **Tower property:** if $\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}$, then
  $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\mid\mathcal{H}]
  =\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{H}]$.
- **Total expectation:**
  $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]]=\mathbb{E}[X]$.
- **Independence:** if $X$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$, then
  $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ a.s.

#### Laws of total probability and total expectation

For a countable partition $(B_i)_i$ with $\mathbb{P}(B_i)>0$,

$$
\mathbb{P}(A)=\sum_i\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i)\mathbb{P}(B_i),
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_i\mathbb{E}[X\mid B_i]\mathbb{P}(B_i).
$$

Equivalently, if $Y$ is discrete,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(X\in A)
  &=\sum_y\mathbb{P}(X\in A\mid Y=y)\mathbb{P}(Y=y),\\
\mathbb{E}[g(X)]
  &=\sum_y\mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid Y=y]\mathbb{P}(Y=y).
\end{aligned}
$$

For a general conditioning variable, sums become integrals against its law $\mu_Y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(X\in A)
  &=\int K_{X\mid Y}(y,A)\,\mu_Y(dy),\\
\mathbb{E}[g(X)]
  &=\int \mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid Y=y]\,\mu_Y(dy).
\end{aligned}
$$

If $Y$ has density $f_Y$, then $\mu_Y(dy)=f_Y(y)dy$. If a conditional density also exists, the
continuous law of total probability is often written

$$
f_X(x)=\int f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)\,dy.
$$

### Probability notation versus ML/RL notation

ML and RL notation usually emphasizes how a sample is generated. It is shorthand for integration
against the corresponding probability measure, not a different definition of expectation.

| Probability language                             | Common ML/RL notation                                               | Meaning                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $\int f(x)\,\mu(dx)$                             | $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)]$                                        | Integrate $f$ against the law denoted informally by $p$.                                                         |
| The policy kernel $\pi(s,da)$                    | $a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)$                                             | Sample an action from the distribution assigned to state $s$.                                                    |
| $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mu_0}^{\pi}}[F(\tau)]$ | $\mathbb{E}_\pi[F(\tau)]$ or $\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\pi}[F(\tau)]$ | Integrate over the trajectory law constructed above; the environment and initial distribution are often omitted. |
| $\int f(s,a)d^\pi(ds)\pi(da\mid s)$              | $\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^\pi,\,a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}[f(s,a)]$          | Integrate under the state-occupancy law and then the policy.                                                     |
| $\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$                               | $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(x_i)$                                    | Approximate the expectation by a Monte Carlo sample average.                                                     |

For finite $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$, the occupancy-measure row becomes

$$
\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^\pi,\,a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}[f(s,a)]
=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d^\pi(s)
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\mid s)f(s,a).
$$

For example, the value of a policy can be written compactly as

$$
V^\pi(s)
= \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t R_t
  \;\middle|\; S_0=s\right].
$$

The subscript $\pi$ therefore does not mean that the policy is the only random object. It names the
policy-dependent trajectory law while suppressing the initial distribution, transition kernel, and
possibly a reward kernel.

### A rigorous definition of an MDP

These ingredients now let us define an infinite-horizon discounted Markov decision process. An MDP
consists of:

- a measurable state space $(\mathcal{S},\mathscr{S})$;
- a measurable action space $(\mathcal{A},\mathscr{A})$;
- a transition kernel $P(ds'\mid s,a)$ from $\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{S}$;
- a measurable expected reward $r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}$; and
- a discount factor $\gamma\in[0,1)$.

An RL problem additionally specifies an initial-state distribution $\mu_0$ and a policy. A
stationary stochastic policy is a kernel $\pi(da\mid s)$ from states to actions. If rewards are
random, $r$ can be replaced by a reward kernel, with $r(s,a)$ denoting its conditional mean.

For finite spaces, $P(s'\mid s,a)$ is an action-dependent transition matrix and
$\pi(a\mid s)$ is a probability vector, so
$\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)=1$ and $\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)=1$.

Let $\mathcal{H}_t=\sigma(S_0,A_0,\ldots,S_t,A_t)$ be the history available at time $t$. The
controlled Markov property is

$$
\mathbb{P}(S_{t+1}\in B\mid\mathcal{H}_t)
=P(B\mid S_t,A_t)
\quad\text{a.s.}
$$

Together, $\mu_0$, $\pi$, and $P$ induce the trajectory law
$\mathbb{P}_{\mu_0}^{\pi}$ through the iterated-kernel construction above. Let
$\mathbb{P}_s^\pi$ denote the law obtained by taking the initial distribution to be the point mass
$\delta_s$. The value function is then rigorously

$$
V^\pi(s)
=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_s^\pi}
\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t r(S_t,A_t)\right].
$$

Applying the tower property and conditioning on the first action and next state gives the Bellman
expectation equation

$$
V^\pi(s)
=\int_\mathcal{A}\pi(da\mid s)
\left[
r(s,a)+\gamma\int_\mathcal{S}P(ds'\mid s,a)V^\pi(s')
\right].
$$

For finite state and action spaces, both integrals become sums:

$$
V^\pi(s)
=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\mid s)
\left[
r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'\in\mathcal{S}}
P(s'\mid s,a)V^\pi(s')
\right].
$$

Thus the familiar RL notation is grounded in three measure-theoretic operations: kernels construct
the trajectory law, conditional expectations express available information, and iterated
expectations produce the Bellman recursion.
